After painstakingly reassembling the wreckage of TWA Flight 800 in 1996, the NTSB concluded
the center-wing fuel tank had ignited, most likely after a short circuit in a wire bundle which led
to a spark in the fuel-gauge sensor.
From this study a new acronym was coined which is widely used in the Aerospace
Industry “EWIS“ Electrical Wiring Interconnection Systems (EWIS).

Wire Stripping
Risk & Importance of Wire Stripping
When working with aircraft wiring, proper techniques are crucial for even the simplest
tasks, such as stripping insulation from a wire. This ensures the quality and functionality
of the installation. The most important aspects of stripping wire are:
• Identify the wire type and choose the proper stripping tool.
• If applicable, adjust the tool to avoid nicking, cutting, or otherwise damaging the strands.
• Do not strip off too much insulation.
• Inspect the conductor for any damage after stripping.

Wire Stripping
Identification
Wire and cable insulation materials vary widely, each offering properties tailored to specific
applications. In the aerospace industry, some of the most commonly used materials include:
• Tefzel (BMS 13-60, MIL-W-22759): Preferred, lightweight, excellent
thermal/chemical resistance, good for hot/cold conditions, often the standard.
• Polyimide (PI) (MIL-W-81044): High-temperature rated, used in demanding
applications, often with other materials.
• Crosslinked Polyalkene (XLETFE) (BMS 13-48): Another common
lightweight, durable type.
• MIL-W-5086/7078 Older standard, nylon-coated, resistant to hydrocarbons
(fuel/oil).
• MIL-W-16878 General-purpose, medium, and high-temperature hookup
wires, with PTFE or FEP insulation.

Identification
• American Wire Gauge (AWG) is a standardized system for
sizing electrical wires in North America.
• A higher AWG number indicates a smaller wire diameter
with the smallest common size being 32 AWG.
• A lower number (like 0, 00, 000, 0000) signifies a larger,
thicker wire, and largest 0000 AWG (4/0).
• This system defines wire dimensions, with specific gauges
carrying different current capacities, crucial for preventing
overheating and ensuring safety, as thicker wires (lower
AWG) handle more current.

Blade Information
• Die-type blades incorporate a counter-bored hole sized to the insulation and an inner
cutting hole sized to the conductor. This varies depending on insulation type and
thickness.
• Knife-type stripping blades have form-ground holes which penetrate thicker and
oversized insulation like a knife.

Wire Stripping
Inspection
• If you use the incorrect blade for the wire insulation type, it may result in damage.
• Insulation shouldn’t have nicks, cuts, or other damage.
• Slight scuffing (dull/rubbed appearance) of the insulation surface finish is acceptable.
• Don’t use insulation that’s damaged beyond minor scuffing.

--- PAGE 26 ---
Wire Stripping
Inspection
• If the twist pattern (lay) of the wire strands is disturbed, restore it to the original pattern as
closely as possible. Re-twisted lay is acceptable when no other damage is evident.
• Strands twisted beyond the normal twist pattern put more stress on the individual strands
and may result in conductor breakage.
